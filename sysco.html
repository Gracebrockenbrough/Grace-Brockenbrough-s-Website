The company

Sysco is a broadline foodservice distributor founded in 1967 and headquartered in Houston. It sits between food producers and the places people eat — restaurants, hospitals, schools, hotels and government institutions — and holds roughly 17% of the U.S. market. In FY2025 it generated about $76.3 billion of revenue, employed some 75,000 people and served 730,000 customers through 337 distribution centers.

This is a high-volume, low-margin business, so the whole game is scale and efficiency. Demand is unusually resilient: people eat regardless of the economic cycle. The trade-off is that a mature company with 17% share has limited room to grow organically, which is why acquisitions matter so much to the story.

The Jetro acquisition

In 2026 Sysco agreed to acquire Jetro Restaurant Depot for $29.1 billion — by a wide margin its largest deal ever, expected to close in 2027. Jetro is a cash-and-carry wholesaler: 166 warehouse stores across 35 states serving more than 725,000 independent restaurants and operators who prefer to pick their own inventory and skip delivery fees.

That matters because it is a customer segment Sysco's delivery-based model could never reach. The cash-and-carry market is worth $60–70 billion and Jetro holds an estimated 23–27% of it. The acquisition adds to the existing business rather than competing with it. Jetro will run as a standalone segment keeping its own operating model and leadership, and the two plan to open more than 125 new locations over the next two decades. Company guidance points to roughly $250 million in annualized net cost synergies.

How I valued it

I used a discounted free cash flow to firm (FCFF) model, forecasting FY2026 through FY2030 plus a terminal value, discounted at the weighted average cost of capital.

FCFF rather than a dividend model, because although Sysco is a Dividend King with 55 consecutive years of dividend growth, management has guided that dividend growth will be modest and that buybacks are paused while acquisition debt is paid down. Free cash flow captures the business as it actually is over the forecast period: stable, cash-generative, and growing through the acquisition rather than through distributions.

The discount rate

This is where the acquisition changes the arithmetic. Sysco plans to fund roughly $22 billion of the deal with new debt, which moves the capital structure from about 30% debt / 70% equity to a near-even split. A standalone discount rate would understate the risk equity holders are taking on, so the WACC had to be rebuilt on a post-acquisition basis.

What the model says

Discounting the forecast cash flows and terminal value at 6.19% gives an enterprise value of roughly $65.5 billion. Netting off debt and dividing by 478.18 million shares gives the fair value per share below. The exit multiple method uses Sysco's own 10.88× EV/EBITDA, because no genuinely comparable company exists at its scale.

The downside case

To test the floor, I ran a dividend discount model that strips out every bit of acquisition-driven growth and uses the higher post-acquisition cost of equity — effectively asking what Sysco is worth if the Jetro deal delivers nothing at all.

It returns roughly $68.91 against a $73.24 market price. Over the same five-day window the stock traded between about $71.50 and $75.50, so a gap of that size is within ordinary price movement. The point is not that the DDM is the right valuation — it is that in the worst case an investor buying today is paying roughly fair value for a company with a 55-year dividend growth streak, with the acquisition upside free on top. The model also excludes future buybacks, which management intends to resume.

Recommendation and risks

Strong Buy. Sysco is a dominant, defensive business with strong free cash flow, now opening a distribution channel it could not previously reach. The base case implies about 48% upside on conservative assumptions, and the downside case still holds roughly fair value.

Three risks carry the thesis:

  • Execution. This is the largest acquisition Sysco has ever attempted, and integration at this scale is genuinely hard.
  • Leverage. Roughly $22 billion of new debt in a thin-margin industry. Net debt to EBITDA rises to around 2.9×.
  • Regulation. A transaction this size draws antitrust scrutiny, and Sysco has had an acquisition blocked before.

Each is reflected in the model rather than argued away: conservative revenue assumptions, a post-acquisition levered beta and higher cost of equity in the bear case, and the range of outcomes in the sensitivity grid above.
